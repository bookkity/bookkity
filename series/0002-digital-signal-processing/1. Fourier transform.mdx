The Fourier transform is one of the most important mathematical tools in modern science and engineering. Its main purpose is to take a complicated real-world signal and decompose it into fundamental building blocks that can then be analyzed much more easily.

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) and closely related transforms, such as the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) and discrete cosine transform (DCT), have applications ranging from audio and image processing and compression (MP3, Opus, Vorbis, etc.) to telecommunication (OFDM/OFDMA in Wi-Fi, 5G NR, LTE), medical imaging (MRI), astronomy (radio interferometry), and many other fields.

Unfortunately, computing the discrete Fourier transform directly is computationally expensive, especially for large input signals.

We can see why by taking a look at its definition.
$$
X_k = \sum_{m = 0}^{N - 1} x_m e^{-i 2\pi \frac{km}{N}}
$$
To compute the full discrete Fourier transform, we need $O(n^2)$ operations. As we can see in the table below, a buffer containing 32,768 samples already requires over one billion summation terms. That's not even a full second of audio at 48 kHz!

|     Input size ($n$) | Operations ($n^2$) |
| -------------------: | -----------------: |
|           $2^5 = 32$ |              1,024 |
|          $2^7 = 128$ |             16,384 |
|          $2^9 = 512$ |            262,144 |
|     $2^{11} = 2,048$ |          4,194,304 |
|     $2^{13} = 8,192$ |         67,108,864 |
|    $2^{15} = 32,768$ |      1,073,741,824 |
|   $2^{17} = 131,072$ |     17,179,869,184 |
|   $2^{19} = 524,288$ |    274,877,906,944 |
| $2^{20} = 1,048,576$ |  1,099,511,627,776 |

Modern computers are powerful, but not that powerful. On my CPU, computing the discrete Fourier transform of a single containing 2,048 elements takes roughly 33 ms using the implementation below. At a sampling rate of 48 kHz, those 2,048 samples represent approximately 42.7 ms of audio. This leaves very little headroom for any other processing, making real-time applications impractical with this approach.

```rs
pub fn naive_discrete_fourier_transform(input: &[Complex], output: &mut [Complex]) {
    assert!(input.len() == output.len());

    let n = input.len();

    for k in 0..n {
        let mut sum = Complex::ZERO;

        for m in 0..n {
            let angle = -2.0 * PI * (k * m) as f32 / n as f32;
            let twiddle = Complex::new(angle.cos(), angle.sin());

            sum += twiddle * input[m];
        }

        output[k] = sum;
    }
}
```

Obviously, we could manually vectorize the code and try to make it as CPU-friendly as possible, but that wouldn't fundamentally change much. Even a highly optimized implementation would still struggle with real-time processing of moderately sized buffers.

Fortunately, as the need arose, clever mathematicians and engineers came up with much more efficient approaches. These approaches are collectively known as the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). We'll start by looking at the so-called Cooley–Tukey algorithm.

The algorithm was generalized and popularized by James W. Cooley and John W. Tukey in their 1965 paper, but, as it turns out, its origins go back much further. Carl Friedrich Gauss had already derived a similar algorithm in an unpublished manuscript from 1805, predating even Joseph Fourier's work on Fourier series. Although Gauss's manuscript was eventually published posthumously, his algorithm remained largely unknown for many years.

Other mathematicians independently developed algorithms based on similar principles, including Carl Runge in 1903 and Danielson and Lanczos in 1942.

We will embark on a journey similar to them. 

Let's take another look at the definition and see if anything interesting catches our eye: 
$$
X_k = \sum_{m = 0}^{N - 1} x_m e^{-i 2\pi \frac{km}{N}}
$$

If we're familiar with linear algebra, we might recognize this as a dot product between the input vector $x_m$ and a vector of complex exponentials $e^{-i 2\pi \frac{km}{N}}$. Strictly speaking, the complex inner product is defined with complex conjugation, so the second vector would contain $e^{i 2\pi \frac{km}{N}}$ instead. However, we'll ignore this distinction for now and treat the expression as an ordinary dot product for simplicity.

In vector notation, we can write it as follows:
$$
X_k = \begin{bmatrix} x_0 & x_1 & \dots & x_{N - 1} \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ e^{-i 2\pi k/N} \\ \vdots \\ e^{-i 2\pi k(N - 1) / N} \end{bmatrix}
$$

If we've ever taken the time to understand linear algebra intuitively, we might recognize that this equation measures how closely our signal aligns with a particular test function.

That's it. The Fourier transform, both discrete and continuous, is essentially a procedure that compares a signal against a family of test functions and tells us how strongly each one is represented in the original signal.

There's also another way to look at it. If we've worked with complex numbers or are familiar with Euler's formula,
$$
e^{ix} = cos(x) + i sin(x)
$$
we might recognize that we're multiplying our signal by a rotating complex exponential.

In a sense, we're finding the balance point of all these rotating vectors. If they point in roughly the same direction, they reinforce each other. If they point in different directions, they tend to cancel each other out. This also explains why certain frequencies produce large DFT coefficients while others produce values close to zero. 

To come up with an efficient algorithm, we'll have to focus on the exponential part of the equation. We don't want to make any assumptions about the input signal, as that could make our algorithm unreliable in certain real-world scenarios.

Let's try to plot $e^{-i 2\pi \frac{km}{N}}$ for different integer values of $km$, using $N = 8$, and see what happens. 

![alt](/series/0001/01/roots-of-unity.svg)

We got a circle! These points are called the _roots of unity_. More specifically, they are the solutions to the polynomial equation $z^N - 1 = 0$.

If we're familiar with complex numbers, we'll probably recognize that multiplication by $i$ rotates a point in the complex plane by 90 degrees counterclockwise.

The same thing happens if the roots of unity. Multiplying by $\omega = e^{-i \frac{2\pi}{N}}$ rotates us from one root of unity to the next.

With that in mind, we can observe two important properties:
- First, for an even $N$, rotating $\frac{N}{2}$ times takes us to the exact opposite side of the unit circle, In other words, we only need to consider half of the roots of unity; the other half are simply their negatives. This is called half-period symmetry or opposite-root property.
$$\omega^{k + N / 2} = -\omega^k$$
- Second, rotating $N$ times brings us right back to where we started. After all, that's a full 360-degree rotation, which leaves the point unchanged. This is called periodicity.
$$\omega^{k + N} = \omega^k$$
